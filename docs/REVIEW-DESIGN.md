# A review that feels like a conversation

A person should be able to understand the choice, give a useful answer, check what
was recorded, and know who will act on it. A diagram is supporting material, not a
substitute for that conversation.

## What changed

- Begin with the decision and a short explanation of what the review is for.
- Ask one question at a time. Let people answer, say they are not sure, or move on.
- Show the suggested next step beside the question. Use a direct source link and a
  separate view for earlier responses. Avoid stacks of expandable sections.
- Collect a written answer separately from an earlier agreement response. Keep old files.
- Show all answers before saving. Missing answers remain open for discussion.
- Keep Save my answers visible. Explain that downloading does not send the file.
- Treat votes as a way to focus discussion. The owner still makes and records the decision.

These are design choices informed by the sources below. Passing browser checks proves
that the controls work; it does not prove that every reader finds the review natural.
The next acceptance check is an observed review with the decision owner.

The left navigation follows three steps: understand the decision, give your view,
and check your answers. Maps and sources sit under supporting material. The top
bar keeps the save or finish action, named icons for display switches, and one Tools menu.
The switches show an on or off state. Extra box details appear only when the current map
has them. Word meanings open a separate list of definitions supplied with the review.
A box always shows its full description when opened. Map views appear only
when someone chooses Explore the map.

## In a room

Give everyone the same decision and a few facts. Ask people to write a response before
hearing the group. Compare responses in pairs or small groups, then bring the differences
back to the room. **Tools → Print the questions** provides a handout with space for
individual answers and the final choice. Use votes to find points worth discussing. Have the owner state the
choice, the reason, and what remains unknown. Record it in plain words.

This draws on [1-2-4-All](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-2-4-all), which moves
from individual reflection to pairs, small groups and the whole room. Decisioncraft's
file review supports individual contributions and a later comparison; it does not
replace a group discussion.

## On a screen

Use one focused question, with its reason close by. Show the recommendation beside the question. Open supporting sources in a separate
view when someone wants to inspect the basis for a claim. Let them go back. Before they finish, show their answers
and the next action. Do not turn uncertainty into agreement or imply that saving submits
a decision.

[GOV.UK's form guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/form-structure)
recommends knowing why each question is needed and starting with one thing per page.
Its [writing guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/writing-for-user-interfaces)
treats a form as a conversation and recommends fixing the interaction when it needs too
much explanation. This informed the question sequence and the visible saving action.

[Google's decision methods](https://designsprintkit.withgoogle.com/methodology/phase4-decide)
include silent review, voting and action planning. They informed the separation between
an individual's response, a signal about importance, and the owner's final decision.

## Language rules

Ask for a judgment a person can give, not confirmation of a technical term. Give the
few facts that affect the choice. Say what is known, what is proposed and what has not
been checked. Use short source titles; keep file locations in source details. Ask one
thing at a time, and explain how the answer will be used.

For example, ask "What would you need to see before sharing a draft?" rather than
"What must a person check before sharing a draft?" when the purpose is to learn the
owner's acceptance criteria. Do not write an answer on their behalf.

## Keep the canvas useful

The original canvas reference put the work and its connections at the centre. Keep
that quality while making review actions clearer. A stage spine uses distinct colours;
current and proposed work can be compared side by side. Selecting a box highlights
its named connections, and the details explain each one. Extra lines can be switched
on together. Review panels refit the drawing into the space that remains. On smaller
screens, the left navigation gives way while the right panel is open.

Customer actions use a horizontal timeline. Service work and system handoffs use lanes.
Alternative ways forward use a branching view. New drafts choose suitable maps from the
material by default; this choice is model-backed and still needs a person's review.
Named relationships must come from the material, rather than positions in a diagram.
